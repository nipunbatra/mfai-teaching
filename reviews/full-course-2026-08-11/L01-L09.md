## Lecture notes

### L1 — Why mathematics for AI?

- Replaced manufactured “mystery” and payoff language with concrete examples and direct explanations; presented the recurring five-stage pipeline as a useful modelling view rather than a universal description of every AI system.
- Qualified the gradient-descent and language-model comparisons. Corrected the claim that every loss is a negative log-likelihood by naming counterexamples such as hinge losses and regularization terms.
- Residual low-confidence issue: 34 slides is short against the usual 50–80-slide guide, but this first-day orientation deliberately leaves time for the live demo, discussion, logistics, and checkpoint. It needs an instructor timebox rather than more content.

### L2 — Floating-point arithmetic

- Corrected the language-specific overflow example: signed overflow is undefined in C, whereas fixed-width NumPy integer arithmetic wraps. Tightened the guidance on floating-point equality, relative error, division by zero, NaNs, stable log-softmax, and the relationship between losses and likelihoods.
- Added worked preparation for fixed-point quantization, mixed precision with float32 accumulation and loss scaling, and compensated summation. Qualified the Kahan guarantee and removed the misleading claim that mixed-precision noise simply averages away.
- Residual low-confidence issue: hardware support and preferred low-precision formats change quickly; the mathematical treatment is stable, but current accelerator-specific details should remain spoken context rather than hard-coded advice.

### L3 — Vectors

- Corrected orthogonality versus statistical independence, the terminology for zero cosine similarity, and the domain of the angle definition. Scoped high-dimensional near-orthogonality to independent isotropic random vectors and noted that learned embeddings can be anisotropic.
- Added a deliberate caveat to the toy recommender: a missing rating is not a numerical zero, and practical systems usually need biases, centering, or latent factors. Replaced exhibit/story phrasing with direct mathematical transitions.
- Residual low-confidence issue: the recommender table still uses zeros as visible placeholders; the adjacent caveat now prevents the intended classroom example from being mistaken for an imputation rule.

### L4 — Matrices

- Distinguished a dense layer from attention, normalization, and residual paths, and stated that both weights and biases are learned. Corrected the composition claim: affine maps alone collapse to one affine map; nonlinearities prevent that algebraic collapse.
- Added an explicit coordinate-map versus image-rendering calculation, including inverse mapping and interpolation, and a slide on what the dense matrix picture leaves out. Qualified solve-versus-inverse advice and removed machine-specific speed claims.
- Replaced non-reproducible fixed Hilbert-matrix error values with code that prints platform-dependent results and a careful conditioning conclusion. Added a purposeful bridge to L5 and the tutorial.
- Residual low-confidence issue: the image-warp treatment is intentionally one-dimensional and conceptual; a full two-dimensional resampling implementation would distract from the linear-map objective.

### L5 — Eigendecomposition

- Scoped the historical PageRank claim and distinguished real-eigenvalue scaling/sign from the rotation-scaling interpretation of a complex-conjugate pair. Made the power-method proof's diagonalizable/eigenbasis assumption explicit.
- Corrected the false claim that float32 rounding rescues the exact start `x0 = (1, -1)`; the illustrated operations are exactly representable, so that start remains in the subdominant eigendirection. Qualified sparse/implicit-matrix claims and the orthonormal-basis statement for repeated eigenspaces.
- Reframed the defective-matrix slide to identify exactly which diagonalization arguments fail rather than implying that all spectral reasoning disappears.
- Residual low-confidence issue: complex eigenpairs receive only geometric intuition; the course does not yet develop complex vector spaces formally.

### L6 — SVD and PCA

- Replaced the literal “rotation–stretch–rotation” description for rectangular matrices with orthonormal input and output coordinate frames, and clarified thin versus full SVD.
- Made low-rank usefulness conditional on singular-value decay. Corrected the PCA implementation claim: common software may use direct or randomized SVD or a covariance-based solver, so it is false that the covariance matrix is never formed.
- Tightened centering, PCA/SVD equivalence, randomized SVD, and summary language; removed claims that most matrices are inherently dominated by only a few directions.
- Residual low-confidence issue: the LoRA parameter-reduction headline is a paper- and architecture-specific illustration, not a general guarantee; the slide cites Hu et al. (2021), and the instructor should keep that qualification audible.

### L7 — Calculus and Taylor approximation

- Defined the derivative as the coefficient of the best local affine approximation rather than equating it with the whole linearization. Scoped the local-parabola minimum test to the nondegenerate case `f''(x*) > 0` and added the flat `x^4` exception.
- Corrected the Taylor-convergence story: smoothness alone is insufficient; for analytic functions the nearest complex singularity controls the radius. Replaced the claim that libraries run a Taylor loop for cosine with range reduction plus carefully designed polynomial approximations.
- Qualified composition and first-order-training claims and removed promissory, payoff, and “one basin” language around convexity.
- Residual low-confidence issue: complex singularities are invoked to state the correct radius-of-convergence principle before complex analysis is taught; the `log(1+x)` example makes the operational point without requiring a proof.

### L8 — Gradients

- Qualified the `0.5` classifier boundary as the usual equal-cost threshold rather than a universal decision rule. Stated that the gradient is normal to a smooth level set only at regular points where the gradient is nonzero.
- Softened universal claims about saddles and plateaus, and distinguished first-order optimizers from algorithms that use more than a gradient.
- Replaced canned “story” and payoff language with direct transitions from the worked derivatives to the general rule.
- Residual low-confidence issue: the finite valley example is illustrative rather than a taxonomy of every plateau mechanism; the revised wording now says so.

### L9 — Jacobians and Hessians

- Added the regularity condition for Hessian symmetry: the relevant second partial derivatives must be continuous. Distinguished the quadratic Taylor model from the actual local geometry.
- Corrected the serious zero-eigenvalue inference. A positive-semidefinite Hessian with a zero eigenvalue does not classify a critical point; the deck now contrasts `x^2 + y^4` and `x^2 - y^4`, which share the same Hessian at the origin but have different behavior.
- Added a global-map/local-linear calculation and an explicit full-Hessian storage estimate. Qualified large-model and higher-order-tensor claims and removed “dirty secret,” cross-examination, mystery, and payoff language.
- Residual low-confidence issue: this is the densest deck in the block. The 62-page rebuilt PDF is visually legible, but the optional tensor material may need to move to reading if the cohort needs more time on Hessian classification.
